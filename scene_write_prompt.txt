THE PRODUCT IS THE HERO: Choose colours, a background and props that match how the product is really used and that support it, like a complete cinematic ad-film setup, so the product is presented as well as it can be. Every colour, surface, prop and light in the scene exists for one reason: to make the product the hero, the first thing the eye lands on, the sharpest, the richest in colour and the best lit element in the frame. Nothing else may compete with it. The product must stand out from its theme and background by contrast (dark on light, light on dark), never merge into them. The twelve hero rules H1 to H12 apply to every product of every category, size and material, from the smallest item to one that fills a room, and to every way a product can sit: resting on a surface, standing on its base, hanging, draped or spread out. For a large product the background is the room and the surfaces around it, and the same rules apply to walls, floor and surroundings. Your scene must pass all twelve. Whenever creativity and a hero rule pull in different directions, the hero rule wins.

H1. Read the product first, then build outward. Before choosing any scene, read the product on five properties: its lightness (dark, mid or light), its colour strength (colourful, muted, neutral, metallic or transparent), its pattern (plain or patterned), its finish (matte, satin, glossy or sparkling) and its form (flat, upright, soft or rigid). Name its three colours: the main colour that covers most of it, the secondary colour, and the accents. Name its most important face: the view in which its design, craftsmanship and value read best. Derive every decision (surface, props, palette, camera, light) from these readings, so the scene is made for this product and could not be moved onto another product without changing.

H2. Match at every scale. Show the product only where it is really used, kept or displayed, and make the match hold at every scale at once: the kind of place, the exact spot within it, the surface it rests on, the objects beside it, the time of day, the light that place really has at that hour, the season and the occasion. Anything that would not be found in that exact spot, with this product, at that moment, does not appear. Test from the largest scale to the smallest (place, spot, surface, props, light) and reject the scene if it fails at any one. Where the product is used in more than one place, choose a place that belongs specifically to this product and its price tier, never one that would suit most other products just as well. The scene must read as that place at a glance even without the product, and the product must look as though it has always belonged there, never placed on a set.

H3. Build the scene in three zones: a surface under the product, a mid-ground beside and behind it, and a far background, each the real material of that place. All three must contrast with the product, and the far background is the calmest. The zones may differ from one another in tone to give depth, but never by more than each differs from the product. Directly around the product keep one continuous, calm field: no line, edge, seam or object between zones may pass behind the product's silhouette or touch its outline, so the silhouette is always read against a single tone, with a margin of calm tone on every side.

H4. Match the colours. The background and props come from the product's own world, the place and the things of its intended use, and their colours harmonise with the product's own. Build the whole scene on one colour family plus one deep or light neutral, never on three or more competing colours. Use either the product's own colour family in a much deeper, much lighter or more muted version, or a quiet complementary colour that flatters it. The product's main colour appears nowhere else in the frame at a competing strength; its secondary colour may appear only far from it and muted; only its accent may be echoed, and only by the accent of the palette. Never use a colour that clashes with the product, a loud colour that pulls attention from it, or a colour that repeats any of the product's hues at the same strength. Every tone is the real colour of a real material that belongs in that place, never chosen for mood, fashion or luxury alone.

H5. Contrast by property. Choose the dominant tone of each zone so that every edge of the product reads clearly, checked edge by edge (top, bottom, left and right) against the zone behind it, with a clear step in lightness at every edge.
- By lightness: a dark product sits on a light or mid tone; a light or pale product sits on a deep tone; a mid-tone product sits on a tone clearly deeper (by default) or clearly lighter than itself.
- By colour: a colourful product sits on a calmer tone that lets its own colour lead; a multi-coloured product sits on one calm tone that contains none of its colours; a product with coloured stones, enamel or print sits on a background that contains none of those hues, so each colour appears only on the product.
- By material: a metal never sits on a tone from its own colour family or on another metallic surface, but on a deeper matte tone that lets its shine and colour read; a transparent or glass product sits on a mid or dark tone so its shape and shine are visible.
- By pattern: a patterned or printed product gets a plain background with no pattern at all; a plain product may sit on a subtle texture finer or coarser than its own, so the two never read as one.
- By finish: a glossy or sparkling product sits in matte surroundings; a matte product may have a little sheen around it, never more than its own.
A product of mixed properties is judged by the property of its largest area, and each smaller part is protected by the colour rule. Pale, near-white tones are never the dominant tone unless the product is dark and needs a light ground to read. The background is never the product's own colour, pattern, texture or material, and no part of the product merges into what is behind it.

H6. Temperature, second to lightness. After lightness and saturation are settled, give the surroundings a mild opposite temperature: a warm-coloured product against surroundings that are neutral or slightly cooler, a cool-coloured product against surroundings that are neutral or slightly warmer. The difference is mild, never a clash, and never overrides the single colour family of H4.

H7. Hierarchy on five counts. The product is first on all five: the most saturated, the most detailed, the largest clear area of the frame, the sharpest, and the best lit. The background is always softer, calmer, less saturated and less busy than the product, calmest directly around the product and allowed richer detail only towards the edges of the frame, so the eye travels inward to the product. Props are fewer than three, each smaller than the product, duller or darker than it, placed to the side or behind it, partly out of focus, never in the product's own colour, never touching or overlapping it, never between the product and the camera, never of the product's own category, and never looking like something included with the product.

H8. Reflections. Whatever surrounds a reflective product appears in it. So the surroundings near any metal, stone, glass, glossy or polished product are calm, free of bright or busy objects, and show in its reflections only the soft real tones of that place: never clutter, never a window or lamp as a hard shape, and never anything that is not in the scene.

H9. Position, view and space. The product faces the camera with its most important face, and its key detail sits in the sharpest and best-lit part of the frame. It is shown at its true real-world size relative to the scene; a small product fills the frame by a closer camera, never by being enlarged. It is never cropped and never touches the frame edge; a margin of calm tone surrounds it, and one side of the frame carries clean, uncluttered space in the dominant tone where product details or offer text can be added later.

H10. Palette. Name a dominant tone, a secondary tone and one accent, each the specific real colour of a real material in that place. The dominant tone fills most of the frame and carries the contrast against the product. The secondary tone belongs to the surface or the mid-ground and sits close to the dominant tone in family. The accent is no more than a touch; it may echo one of the product's accent colours to tie the product to the scene, but never dominates, competes, or touches the product.

H11. Light as the highlighter. Light the product a step brighter than its surroundings, from a direction that sculpts its texture and material; the brightest point in the frame is on the product, and the background is lit a step below it. Shadows fall away from the product in one consistent direction, with a soft contact shadow that seats it on the surface. Metal, stones, glass and glossy surfaces show the sparkle and true colour they have in real life under this light, never flatter and never brighter than in the reference, and the light never changes how the product's colour reads. No bright highlight, light source, window, lamp or reflection sits beside or behind the product where it would compete with it, and no glow, haze or halo surrounds it.

H12. Hero tests and repair. At one glance the product must be the first thing seen. Check that it is still the clearest shape when the image is imagined blurred, still readable at phone-thumbnail size, still in contrast in black and white, still separated when the product is imagined as one flat grey against the greys touching its edges, and that the scene looks incomplete when the product is imagined covered. If any background element or prop draws the eye first, darken, blur, desaturate or remove it. If any product edge merges with what is behind it, change the tone behind that edge. If the scene would work equally well for a different product, change the scene. If no scene for this product passes every rule, simplify towards a calmer single real surface of that place, deeper or lighter than the product, never towards a studio backdrop; a scene that fails any rule is never passed. State the palette in one sentence, in this form: "The background is [specific colour of a real material], a [deeper/lighter], softer and less saturated tone than the product's [colour], so the product stands out with every edge clearly visible."

READING THE CONTEXT: The category and subcategory tell you what kind of product this is and so where it is really used (rule H2). The description was written from a quick look at the same photo; if it disagrees with the photo, trust the photo. A set or pair of several pieces is one hero: keep every piece fully in the frame.

A NEW SCENE EVERY TIME: The CONTEXT lists the scenes already used for this subcategory. Yours must differ from every one of them in the kind of place or the spot within it, and in the dominant colour family, and also in at least one of the surface material, the light or the camera angle. Do not rename, recolour or lightly rearrange an earlier scene. First note the places, dominant colours and times of day already used, then choose a combination none of them uses; vary the time of day, season and occasion too where the product's real use allows. When many scenes exist, look for real places and occasions of this product not yet used, and return to a used place only with a clearly different spot, palette and light. The hero rules always win over novelty. With no earlier scenes, choose the best scene for this product.

HOW TO WRITE IT: The image model sees only your words, so be exact: real colours and real materials (a deep teal glazed tile, not a nice background), plain directions (soft light from the upper left, camera at table height and slightly above the product), sizes relative to the product. A prop "behind" the product means farther back and to one side, never overlapping or touching the product's outline in the picture. Before you answer, check your draft against the twelve hero rules and the palette sentence, and correct anything that fails.
